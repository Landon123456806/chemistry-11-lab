## Question 1: Physical vs Chemical Change (Dry Ice Observations)

**Tell whether the following would be a physical or chemical change and explain your reasoning:**

### a) Dry ice on the lab bench
**Answer: PHYSICAL CHANGE**
- **Reasoning:** Dry ice (solid CO₂) subliming on the lab bench is a physical change. The carbon dioxide molecules remain the same; only the state of matter changes from solid directly to gas. No new substances are formed, and the process is reversible if the gas is recondensed under pressure and low temperature.

### b) Dry ice in the water
**Answer: PHYSICAL CHANGE**
- **Reasoning:** When dry ice is placed in water, it sublimes (solid → gas) while the water remains water. The dry ice and water do not react chemically. The "smoking" effect is simply CO₂ gas escaping from the subliming dry ice and cooling the surrounding water vapor, causing it to condense into visible droplets. No new chemical compounds are formed.

### c) Dry ice in water with dish soap
**Answer: PHYSICAL CHANGE**
- **Reasoning:** Adding dish soap creates bubbles, but this is still a physical change. The soap acts as a surfactant, trapping the CO₂ gas bubbles in a foam. The dry ice still sublimes (solid → gas), and the soap simply stabilizes the bubbles mechanically. No chemical reaction occurs between the dry ice, water, and soap.

## Question 2: Physical Evidence of Chemical Reaction

**What physical evidence indicates that a chemical reaction occurred when yeast was added to the hydrogen peroxide?**

**Answer:**

The following physical evidence indicates a chemical reaction occurred:

1. **Rapid Gas Production (Vigorous Bubbling):** Large volumes of oxygen gas (O₂) were rapidly produced, creating foam and bubbles. This is a clear sign of a decomposition reaction breaking down hydrogen peroxide into products.

2. **Temperature Increase:** The temperature rose from 21.3°C to 32.0°C over 120 seconds (ΔT = +10.7°C). This indicates an **exothermic reaction** - energy was released as heat. The yeast catalyzed the decomposition of H₂O₂ into O₂ and H₂O, releasing thermal energy.

3. **Formation of New Substances:** The reaction produced:
   - Oxygen gas (O₂) - evidenced by bubbling
   - Water (H₂O) - absorbs some heat
   - The yeast itself was a catalyst (unchanged but enabled the reaction)

4. **Irreversibility:** Once the reaction occurred, we cannot reverse it simply by cooling. The hydrogen peroxide was consumed and converted into new products, confirming a chemical change.

## Question 3: Physical or Chemical Change - Gas Bubbles

**Is the production of gas bubbles in this experiment a physical change or a chemical change? Explain your reasoning for each part.**

**Answer:**

### Overall Answer: CHEMICAL CHANGE

### Detailed Reasoning:

#### Part 1: Formation of Gas Bubbles - **CHEMICAL CHANGE**
- **Reasoning:** The gas bubbles are composed of oxygen (O₂) gas, which is a NEW SUBSTANCE that did not exist before the reaction. The hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is chemically broken down (decomposed) into oxygen gas and water:
  
  **2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂ ↑**
  
- The yeast (containing the enzyme catalase) speeds up this decomposition reaction. New chemical bonds are broken, and new substances are formed - this is definitively a chemical change.

#### Part 2: Bubbling/Foam Formation - **PHYSICAL CHANGE**
- **Reasoning:** Once the oxygen gas is produced by the chemical reaction, the bubbling and foam formation is a PHYSICAL CHANGE. The gas (O₂) is simply escaping from the liquid and rising to the surface. The dish soap (if used) would trap these bubbles mechanically to create foam, but no new substances are being formed - just a physical rearrangement of existing matter (gas dispersed in liquid).

### Summary:
- **Chemical change:** Production of O₂ from H₂O₂ decomposition
- **Physical change:** The escape and movement of the gas bubbles through the liquid

The experiment demonstrates both types of changes occurring simultaneously.

## Chemical Equation
**2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂↑** (with yeast/catalase catalyst)

The reaction is spontaneous but slow at room temperature. The catalase enzyme in yeast dramatically increases the reaction rate, causing the rapid decomposition and heat release observed in the experiment.
